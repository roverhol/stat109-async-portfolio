Section A — Reading a scatterplot

A1. Describe an association

A scatterplot shows fertilizer dose (x) and plant height in cm (y): the points slope upward and cluster tightly near a line. Describe the association using form, direction, and strength.

A2. Curved vs linear

A scatterplot of age and reading speed shows a clear arc (reading peaks in mid-life and declines at both ends). Is Pearson correlation an appropriate summary? Explain.

Click to reveal answers — Section A — Reading a scatterplot

  1. Form: roughly linear. Direction: positive (more fertilizer → taller plant). Strength: strong (points cluster tightly).

  2. No — Pearson \(r\) measures linear association only. A curved (non-linear) form makes \(r\) a misleading summary of the relationship.

Section B — Interpreting correlation and regression output

B1. Interpret r

R gives cor(exercise_min, resting_hr) = −0.78. Interpret this value in context.

B2. Interpret regression output

R output: (Intercept) 79.1, exercise_min −0.30. Write the fitted equation and interpret the slope.

B3. Point prediction

Using the equation above, predict resting HR for someone who exercises 30 min/day.

B4. Residual computation

A person exercises 30 min/day and has a resting HR of 74 bpm. Using the model above, compute their residual.

Click to reveal answers — Section B — Interpreting correlation and regression output

  1. Strong negative linear association: as daily exercise minutes increase, resting heart rate tends to decrease. The relationship is strong (\(|r|\) close to 1).

  2. \(\hat{\text{resting HR}} = 79.1 - 0.30\,(\text{exercise min})\). Slope: each additional minute of daily exercise is associated with a predicted 0.30 bpm decrease in resting heart rate.

  3. \(\hat{y} = 79.1 - 0.30(30) = 79.1 - 9.0 = 70.1\) bpm.

  4. Residual \(= 74 - 70.1 = 3.9\) bpm — they have a resting HR 3.9 bpm higher than the model predicts.

Section C — CI for slope

C1. CI for slope — fertilizer

For the plant model, confint(fit_plant, 'dose') → [1.7, 2.6]. Interpret in context.

C2. Practical significance

The 95% CI for slope is \([0.001, 0.003]\), not containing 0. Is this association statistically significant? Is it practically significant?

Click to reveal answers — Section C — CI for slope

  1. We are 95% confident that each additional unit of fertilizer dose changes the mean plant height by between 1.7 and 2.6 cm.

  2. Statistically significant (CI excludes 0). Practical significance depends on context — a change of 0.001–0.003 units per 1-unit increase may be too small to matter in practice.

Section D — Mean-response CI vs prediction interval

D1. Identify correct interval

A biologist wants to estimate the average plant height for all plants given exactly 7 units of fertilizer. Should they use interval = 'confidence' or interval = 'prediction'?

D2. Identify correct interval — individual

A researcher wants an interval for one specific new plant given 7 units of fertilizer. Which interval?

D3. Width comparison

At \(x_0 = 7\): CI for mean = \([22.8, 25.6]\); PI for individual = \([13.1, 35.3]\). What accounts for the large difference in width?

Click to reveal answers — Section D — Mean-response CI vs prediction interval

  1. interval = 'confidence' — this estimates where the mean response (average outcome) falls, not an individual plant.

  2. interval = 'prediction' — this covers where one individual future observation is likely to fall.

  3. The PI adds individual-to-individual variability on top of uncertainty in the mean. Even if we knew the mean exactly, individual plants would vary widely around it.
